## Use direct outreach to learn, not to manufacture familiarity

A good first message is relevant, truthful, brief enough to answer, and easy to decline. It says who you are, why this person is a reasonable fit, what you are asking, and how to stop further contact. Personalization must be based on something you actually observed; invented compliments and scraped details damage trust.

## Build a qualified list before writing

Define the behavior or role that makes someone useful to contact. Find people through a legitimate, public source or referral, and check the rules for that source. Do not buy lists or harvest contact details in ways that breach platform terms. Start with a small batch you can research and follow up with thoughtfully.

Track only what you need: source, fit reason, date, response, opt-out, and next step. Avoid collecting sensitive personal information. After an opt-out, keep only the minimum suppression record needed to prevent another contact; delete other notes when you no longer need them.

## Write one specific message

```text
Subject: How do you handle [specific task]?

Hi [name] — I’m [your name], building [plain description]. I noticed [truthful reason this role or person is relevant]. I’m trying to understand how [specific group] handles [recent workflow], especially [one concrete uncertainty].

Would you be open to a 15-minute conversation next week? I’m not asking you to endorse a product. If this is not relevant, no reply is needed and I won’t follow up.

[Name and a way to contact you]
```

If you are selling, say so. Do not call a sales demo “research.” If the product is not ready, do not imply it is available. Ask for one small next step, not a meeting, survey, referral, and purchase all at once.

## Send, listen, and stop

A single polite follow-up can be reasonable when the first message was genuinely relevant and the recipient has not opted out; set the rule in advance. Stop immediately when asked. Do not use tracking pixels or fake “Re:” subjects to create pressure. Measure qualified replies and conversations, not opens. A low reply rate may reflect poor targeting or trust, not proof that nobody has the problem.

Commercial email and privacy rules vary by sender, recipient, message, and country. Check current requirements for each place you contact. For US commercial email, read the FTC’s [CAN-SPAM compliance guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business); it is not a worldwide rulebook.
