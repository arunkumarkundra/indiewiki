## Recruit for experience, not agreement

Invite people who recently encountered the target situation. Explain the topic and time required, and avoid describing your proposed solution before hearing their account. A friendly response to your idea is not evidence of demand.

Ask about a specific recent instance: “Tell me about the last time you prepared that report.” Follow the sequence: what triggered it, what they tried, what tools or people were involved, what was difficult, and what happened next. Ask neutral follow-ups such as “What did you do then?”

## Avoid hypothetical validation

Questions like “Would you use this?” invite politeness and speculation. Prefer evidence of effort or commitment: repeated workarounds, time spent, budget already allocated, or willingness to schedule a next step. No single signal proves a viable business.

Do not turn an interview into a disguised sales pitch. If you show a prototype, ask the participant to complete a task and observe where they hesitate.

## Synthesize carefully

After each conversation, separate direct quotes and observed behavior from your interpretation. Compare across participants and note counterexamples. A handful of interviews helps discover patterns and language; it does not estimate how common those patterns are. If prevalence matters, use an appropriate broader method.

Store only notes you need, explain how you will use them, and remove identifying details when they are not necessary.
