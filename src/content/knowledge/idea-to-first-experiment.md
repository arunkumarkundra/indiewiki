## Start with a person and a recurring situation

Write down who has the problem, when it happens, what they do today, and what makes that workaround costly. “People need better productivity” is too broad to test. “Independent consultants lose billable time assembling a weekly client status report from three tools” gives you a person, trigger, and observable task.

Treat this as a hypothesis. Do not assume that a visible annoyance is urgent, frequent, or worth paying to solve. Record what you know, what you have inferred, and what evidence would change your mind.

## Make the next step cheap

Before building, choose one uncertainty whose answer would change your decision. If you do not know whether the task occurs often, speak with people who recently did it. If you do not know whether a workflow is understandable, show a clickable prototype. If you do not know whether anyone will pay, ask for a concrete commitment with clear terms.

Write the experiment in advance: audience, question, method, time limit, observations to record, and decision rule. A useful test can produce a “no” that saves weeks.

## Decide what the result means

Separate observations from interpretation. “Three of five interviewees described exporting a report last Friday” is an observation; “the market is large” is not. A small sample can reveal vocabulary and workflow, but it cannot establish prevalence.

Choose a follow-up based on remaining uncertainty. Keep the notes and decision so a future contributor can see why the project moved forward or stopped.
