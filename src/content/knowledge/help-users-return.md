## Start with the job that brings someone back

Retention only makes sense when the product addresses a need that recurs. Identify the user’s reason to return and the natural interval: daily collaboration, a monthly report, or a one-time task may require very different expectations. Do not label occasional use as churn if the product is designed for occasional use.

## Make the next useful step obvious

Help a new user reach a meaningful first outcome with clear onboarding, sensible defaults, and an easy path back to unfinished work. Ask only for information needed at that point. Show empty states that explain what to do next, and make recurring tasks easier without sending unnecessary reminders.

Observe where people stop: setup, first success, repeated workflow, or renewal. Pair event data with support conversations so a metric does not hide why behavior changed. Define cohorts and time windows before comparing retention; product usage can vary by season and acquisition source.

## Earn attention

Use notifications only when they provide timely, relevant value that the user can control. Make frequency and channel understandable and provide a working way to adjust or stop messages. Avoid dark patterns, artificial urgency, and streak mechanics that reward time spent without helping the user.

If users do not return, investigate whether the need recurs, the outcome was valuable, setup is too difficult, or an external change altered demand. A retention chart describes behavior; it does not explain cause by itself.
