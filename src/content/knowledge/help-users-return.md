## Retention means the product keeps solving a recurring job

Retention is not the same as sending more notifications. Users return when the need recurs and the product reliably helps them reach the outcome. If the job is naturally one-time, a high repeat-use rate may be the wrong goal; measure whether the one-time outcome succeeded and whether the user would choose the product again for a similar need.

## Define the return event from the job

Choose an action that signals value. For bookkeeping, it could be “completed a monthly reconciliation,” not “opened the dashboard.” For a one-time tax organizer, completion and successful handoff may matter more than daily active use. Write the event so another person can distinguish real completion from a page view.

Measure cohorts by first meaningful use. For recurring jobs, compare the share that completes the value event in week 1, week 2, month 1, and the expected natural cycle. Show the denominator and cohort dates. New products have small samples; inspect user-level stories rather than overinterpreting a percentage. If customers use the product on different cycles, use a period that matches the job.

## Find the reason people do not return

Talk to users who stopped, when they are willing. Ask what they were trying to accomplish, where the product fit into their workflow, what they did instead, and what changed. Review product events and support reports with the user’s consent and appropriate privacy controls. Common causes include: the product never delivered the first outcome, data had to be entered twice, setup took too long, the next use was months away, trust was lost, or the workflow moved elsewhere.

Do not begin with a discount or reminder sequence. First determine whether the product still has value and whether the person expects to use it again. Fix recurring friction before adding engagement mechanics.

## Help without nagging

Make the next useful action visible after setup. Save progress, show a clear empty state, and remind users about work they chose to track. Let people control frequency, channel, and pause/stop preferences. Notifications should be timely, truthful, and tied to a user benefit; do not manufacture urgency or hide opt-out controls. For email, use a preference center and honor unsubscribes according to applicable rules.

Provide a way to export or recover user work and an easy route to human support. If a product’s value depends on a recurring external event, explain when to return rather than sending generic weekly reminders.

## Run a small retention review

Every cycle, look at: who completed the first outcome; who returned at the natural interval; where the path broke; what users said; which segment retained better; and one change to test. Separate a correlation from a cause—users who use a feature more may already be more motivated. Test the change with a clear comparison and avoid manipulative streaks or dark patterns.
