Most indie founders charge too little. Low prices feel safe, but they attract the most demanding customers, leave no margin for support, and make the product look less serious. Price on the value you create for the customer, not on your costs or on what feels polite.

## Start from value, not cost

Ask: what does the problem cost the customer today? Hours of work, a freelancer, lost sales, mistakes. If your product saves a business ten hours a month, charging a small fraction of that is an easy yes. Your interviews from [validation](/start/validate-your-idea/) should tell you what they spend now. That is your anchor.

Then sanity-check against alternatives: what do competitors charge, and what does the do-it-yourself path cost?

## Pick a model

| Model | Fits | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly or yearly subscription | Ongoing value: SaaS, data, hosting | Churn; offer a yearly plan with a discount |
| One-time purchase | Tools that do one job, templates, games, desktop apps | Revenue stops unless you sell upgrades or new versions |
| Usage-based | APIs, AI features, sending, storage | Customers fear surprise bills; show usage clearly and offer caps |
| Free trial, then paid | Products whose value shows in days | Make sure users reach value during the trial |
| Free plan plus paid tiers | Products that spread through free users | Free users must not cost you much |

## Tiers that make choosing easy

Two or three plans are plenty. A common shape:

- **Starter**: for individuals. The core job, with sensible limits.
- **Pro**: the plan you want most people on. Clearly the best value.
- **Team or Business**: higher limits, collaboration, priority support, invoices.

Separate tiers by something that grows with the value customers get (seats, projects, volume), not by hiding basic features. Show the yearly price as a monthly equivalent with the saving visible.

## Charge more than feels comfortable

Some useful habits from experienced founders:

- **Price at the premium end or the very low end.** The middle is where you compete with everyone.
- **Sell to customers who can afford it first.** A business customer who values the result is easier to serve than many price-sensitive individuals.
- **If nearly everyone says yes, raise the price** for new customers and watch what happens.
- **Do not cut prices to fix weak sales.** First fix the message, the audience or the product.

## Pricing for different countries

Local pricing can open markets where a US-dollar price is out of reach. Merchant-of-record services and app stores can show local currencies and adjust prices by country.

::: india If you are in India
Indian customers often expect lower prices and monthly UPI-friendly payments, while international customers may expect US dollar prices and card payments. Many Indian founders run separate pricing for India and the rest of the world. If you sell to Indian customers yourself, prices usually need to show whether GST is included. See [Accepting payments](/earn/accepting-payments/).
:::

## Raising prices

Raise prices when you have added real value, when demand outstrips your capacity, or when you realise you started too low. Keep existing customers on their old price for a while (or for good) and tell them early. New customers will see only the new price and will not know the difference.
