A product is what you built. An **offer** is what someone is deciding to buy: the outcome, the price, the terms, the proof, the guarantee and the timing, all together. Two founders with the same product can sell very different amounts because of the offer.

## The four levers

A useful frame, popularised by Alex Hormozi in *$100M Offers*, says people value an offer more when:

1. **The outcome is something they badly want.** Sell the result ("invoices paid on time"), not the features ("automated reminders").
2. **They believe they will actually get it.** Proof, a clear explanation of how it works, testimonials from people like them, and a guarantee.
3. **It happens fast.** The first meaningful win comes in minutes or days, not months.
4. **It takes little effort.** Done for them beats done by them. Setup is short; defaults are good.

You can raise value by improving any lever without changing the product at all.

## Apply it to a small software product

| Lever | Weak | Stronger |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Outcome | "Social media scheduler" | "A month of posts planned in one afternoon" |
| Belief | No proof | Three customer screenshots with names, plus a 30-day refund promise |
| Speed | Empty account after sign-up | Sample data and a first post drafted in the first two minutes |
| Effort | Customer imports everything | "Send us your spreadsheet; we import it for you" for the first 20 customers |

## Name the pain precisely

People buy when you describe their problem better than they could. Use the exact phrases from your interviews and support emails on your landing page and in your outreach. Specific beats clever: "Chasing clients for payment on WhatsApp every Friday" lands harder than "Streamline your receivables".

## Narrow the audience until it feels too narrow

"Invoicing for freelance video editors" converts better than "invoicing for everyone", because each visitor in that group sees themselves in it. You can create more narrow offers later for other groups with the same product.

## Make the extras concrete

If you add bonuses (an onboarding call, templates, a migration service), list each one separately with what it does for the buyer. A vague "plus lots of extras" adds nothing.

## Add honest urgency and scarcity

Real reasons to act now work: a launch price for the first 50 customers, a cohort that starts on a date, limited onboarding slots because you do them personally. Fake countdown timers damage trust.

## Respond fast

The speed of your reply to a lead strongly affects whether they buy. Answer trial users, demo requests and pricing questions within hours, not days.

## Checklist for your offer

- [ ] The headline states the outcome in the customer's words.
- [ ] The page shows proof from people like the visitor.
- [ ] The first win comes in the first session.
- [ ] Setup takes minutes, or you do it for them.
- [ ] A simple guarantee or refund policy is visible near the price.
- [ ] Any bonus is listed individually with its value.
- [ ] There is an honest reason to act now.
