Cold outreach is a message to someone who does not know you yet. Done well, it is the most direct way for a founder to find early customers, advisers and partners. Done badly, it is spam. The difference is mostly **who** you write to and **how much the message is about them**.

## Target better, not wider

A perfect email to the wrong person gets no reply. A plain email to exactly the right person often does.

- **Copy who already said yes.** Look at the job titles and companies of your existing users or the people from your interviews. Find more of the same.
- **No users yet?** Ask who suffers most if the problem goes unsolved, and who decides to fix it.
- **Look for timing signals**: a job post for the role your product helps, a recent funding round, a new feature launch, a public complaint about a tool you replace.
- **One hundred good fits beat a thousand random addresses.** Check each one by hand at the start.

## Write something worth reading

Aim for a message someone can read in under a minute. Three to six short sentences for email, two to four for LinkedIn.

1. **Why them.** One line that shows you looked: something specific you share or noticed.
2. **Their problem**, in their words, not your product's features.
3. **What you do**, in one plain sentence, with one piece of proof (a customer, a result, or why you are the right person).
4. **One clear ask**, on its own line, that is easy to say yes to.

Skip the life story, the flattery and the jargon. Write like you talk to a smart friend, and read it out loud before sending.

::: tip Ask for something small
"Would it be useful if I sent a two-minute video showing how this would work for [their company]?" gets more replies than "Can we book a 30-minute call?" Let them choose to go further.
:::

### A template to adapt

```text
Subject: [their company] + [the specific problem]

Hi [name],

Saw [specific thing: their post, job ad, product change].
Teams like yours often lose [time/money] on [problem] because [reason].

I built [product], which [does the job] in [short time]. [Customer or result].

Worth a 2-minute video showing how it would work for [company]?

[Your name]
[Product] — [link]
```

## Send it right

- Send from an address on your own domain, with your name in it, set up with SPF, DKIM and DMARC. See [Email on your custom domain](/setup/custom-domain-email/).
- Use a casual subject line a colleague might write. Short, specific, lower case is fine.
- Keep links to one, and no attachments in the first email.
- Send a small number each day rather than hundreds at once, especially from a new domain.

## Follow up, then let go

Most replies come after a follow-up. Send two to four short follow-ups a few days apart, each adding something useful (a relevant example, a short video, an answer to a likely objection). The last one can politely close the loop: "I'll stop here. If [problem] becomes a priority, I'm one reply away." That last message often gets the most replies.

If someone is not interested, thank them and stop. Do not take silence personally; check back months later if something relevant changes.

## When someone replies

Answer the same day, and get the next step on their calendar while interest is fresh. On every first call, ask what made them reply. Their answer is your next subject line. Ask customers why they bought: they write better copy than you do.

## Low replies? Debug in this order

1. **Right people?** Would these people really feel the problem?
2. **Right companies?** Size, industry, timing.
3. **Subject line.** Is it opened? Try a plainer one.
4. **The message.** Is the first sentence about them? Is the ask small?
5. **Your website and profile.** Do they back up the email in five seconds?
6. **Deliverability.** Are you landing in spam? Test with a few personal addresses at different providers.

If all six look fine and replies are still near zero, the problem may be the product or the offer, not the outreach. See [Make an offer people say yes to](/earn/making-an-offer/).

## Make it a habit

Outreach is the task founders avoid most. Pick a daily number (five, ten), block the time, and track sent, replied and booked in a simple sheet.
