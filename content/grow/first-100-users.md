The first hundred users rarely come from one clever trick. They come from a founder personally reaching people who have the problem, one conversation at a time, while the product improves from what those people say. It feels slow. It is the fastest way to learn.

## Do things that do not scale

Early on, effort that would be absurd at 10,000 users is exactly right:

- Email every new sign-up yourself and ask one question: "What made you try it?"
- Onboard people on a video call and watch where they get stuck.
- Do part of the job by hand behind the scenes until you know what to automate.
- Fix a user's reported bug the same day and tell them personally.

These habits turn early users into fans who tell others, and they show you what to build next.

## Where the first users come from

Pick two of these and do them consistently for a month, rather than trying all of them once.

**People you already know about.** The people you interviewed during [validation](/start/validate-your-idea/), your waitlist, and anyone who asked "tell me when it's ready". Message each one personally on launch.

**Direct outreach.** Find people who match your user exactly and write to them individually. Short, specific and about their problem. See [Cold outreach that gets replies](/grow/cold-outreach/).

**Communities.** Answer questions helpfully in the places your users ask them, and mention your product only where it truly answers the question and the rules allow. See [Communities and Reddit](/grow/communities/).

**Launch sites and directories.** A launch brings a spike; directories bring a trickle that lasts. See [Where to launch](/launch/where-to-launch/).

**Building in public.** Share what you are building, numbers and lessons, on the platform where your users or peers gather. It compounds slowly and builds trust.

**Content and search.** Write pages that answer the exact questions your users search for. Slow to start, strong after six months. See [Content that ranks](/grow/content-and-seo/).

**Partnerships.** Find a product or creator who already serves your users and offer something useful to their audience: an integration, a guide, a discount.

## Copy what works, not what is loud

When a post or message does well, look at why: the hook, the format, the community, the timing. Make another one in the same shape. Keep a simple list of everything you try, with the date and the result, so you are deciding from your own data, not from someone's thread about their overnight success.

## Measure the right thing

Count **activated users**, not sign-ups or visits. Define activation as the first moment a user gets real value: their first report generated, first invoice sent, first file shared. Then track, per channel:

| Channel | Visits | Sign-ups | Activated | Still active after 2 weeks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Example: community answers | 300 | 24 | 15 | 9 |
| Example: launch site | 2,000 | 80 | 22 | 5 |

A channel with fewer visits but more people who stay is the one to double down on.

## When it is not working

If people sign up but do not come back, the problem is the product or the audience, not the marketing. Talk to the people who left. If almost nobody signs up after a month of steady outreach to the right people, the message or the problem may be off. Revisit your interview notes before you add more channels.
