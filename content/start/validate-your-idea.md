Most products fail because nobody needs them badly enough, not because the code was bad. Validation is cheap insurance: a week of conversations can save six months of building the wrong thing.

The goal of this week is not to prove your idea is good. It is to find out the truth as fast as possible, including the truth that you should change the idea.

## The strongest signal there is

Look for people who already go through an awkward, slow or expensive process to get a result. Spreadsheets held together with formulas, five tools glued with copy and paste, a forum thread with 400 replies asking the same question, someone paying a freelancer every month for the same small job. That effort is proof of demand. Your job is to offer the clean version.

Weak signals, by contrast, are compliments ("cool idea!"), sign-ups to a waitlist with nothing else asked, and likes on a post.

## Day 1: write down your guess

Write one paragraph, in this shape:

> **[A specific type of person]** struggles with **[a specific problem]** when **[a situation]**. Today they **[current workaround]**, which costs them **[time, money or risk]**.

Be narrow. "Small businesses" is not a type of person. "Wedding photographers in India who deliver 2,000 photos per event" is. A narrow audience is easier to find, easier to talk to, and easier to sell to. You can widen later.

## Day 2: find where they already talk

List ten places your person spends time online: subreddits, Discord and Slack groups, Facebook groups, forums, LinkedIn hashtags, YouTube comment sections, review sites for the tools they use now. Read the last month of posts in each. Copy every sentence that describes the problem in their own words into a document. These phrases become your landing page copy later.

::: tip Search the complaints
Search Reddit and forums for "[current tool] alternative", "[current tool] too expensive", "how do you manage [problem]" and "I hate [task]". Complaints are better research than praise.
:::

## Days 3 to 5: talk to ten people

Ask for 15 minutes to learn about their work, not to pitch. A short, honest message works: who you are, what you are trying to understand, and that you are not selling anything yet.

In the conversation, ask about the past, not the future. People are reliable about what they did and unreliable about what they would do.

::: steps
1. **"Tell me about the last time you had to [do the task]."** Get the full story: when, why, what tools, how long.
2. **"What was the hardest part?"** Listen for emotion: frustration, fear of mistakes, boredom.
3. **"What have you tried to fix it?"** If they have tried nothing, the problem may not hurt enough.
4. **"What do you pay for that today?"** Money, tools, freelancers, their own hours.
5. **"Who else should I talk to?"** Each good conversation should lead to the next.
:::

Do not describe your solution until the last two minutes, if at all. The moment you pitch, people start being polite.

## Day 6: look for the pattern

Put every conversation in one table: their situation, current workaround, cost, how much it hurts (your honest guess), and anything they asked for. You are looking for:

- **The same pain, in the same words, from different people.** That is your headline.
- **Money or time already being spent.** That is your price anchor.
- **A group that hurts most.** That is your first customer, even if it is a smaller group than you hoped.

If you cannot find a pattern, change one thing (the audience, the problem or the situation) and repeat the week. That is a win, not a failure: you just saved months.

## Day 7: ask for a small commitment

Build the smallest thing that lets someone say yes with action, not words:

- A one-page site with the headline in their words, three bullet points, and a button to pre-order, book a call or join a paid pilot.
- A manual version of the service. Do the job by hand, behind a form, for five people. It feels unscalable. It is the fastest way to learn what the software must do.
- A clickable mockup you walk through on a call, ending with "Would you like to be one of the first five users at [price]?"

Count how many people take the action. Five strangers who pay or book time are worth more than five hundred waitlist sign-ups.

## Before you move on

- [ ] I can describe my user in one sentence that excludes most people.
- [ ] I have talked to at least eight people who have the problem.
- [ ] I have their words for the problem saved in a document.
- [ ] I know what they use or pay for today.
- [ ] At least a few people took a real action: paid, pre-ordered, or gave me time to try a rough version.
